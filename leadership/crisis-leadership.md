# Crisis Leadership

## When to Use

- Company survival is threatened (revenue collapse, competitor attack, key person departure)
- You need to do layoffs or fire executives
- No good options exist — only bad and worse
- Team morale is collapsing under pressure
- You're personally struggling with the weight of leadership
- Decisions need to be made fast with incomplete information

## Peacetime vs. Wartime

The same leader must be two completely different people depending on context. Most leaders only know one mode — and the wrong mode at the wrong time is fatal.

| Peacetime | Wartime |
|-----------|---------|
| Expand market | Survive |
| Build culture | Win at any cost |
| Encourage creativity | Enforce discipline |
| Delegate broadly | Make decisions fast |
| Hire for growth | Cut ruthlessly |
| Long-term focus | Immediate survival |
| "Let's explore options" | "Follow me, now" |

**Peacetime**: Company has clear advantage, expanding market, growth mode.
**Wartime**: Company survival threatened, existential competition, crisis mode.

**The trap**: Peacetime leaders fail in wartime. They're too nice, too slow, too consensus-driven. The company dies while they're still processing options.

**Know which mode you're in. Act accordingly.**

## The Struggle

The Struggle is when you wonder why you started in the first place. When people ask why you don't quit and you don't know the answer. When your employees think you're lying and you think they might be right.

**What it feels like:**
- Self-doubt becomes constant
- Sleep becomes impossible
- Motivation disappears
- Every decision feels wrong
- Isolation despite being surrounded by people

**What helps:**
- Don't put it all on your shoulders — share the burden with people you trust
- This is not checkers; this is chess — there's always a move
- Play long enough and you might get lucky
- Don't take it personally
- Remember: most great founders faced worse and came through

**What doesn't help:**
- Pretending everything is fine
- Isolating yourself
- Seeking silver bullets
- Feeling sorry for yourself (the people losing jobs have it worse)

## Making Decisions Without Good Options

Most leadership decisions in crisis are between bad and worse. There's no good choice — only the best of terrible options.

### The Framework

1. **Don't delay.** Bad news doesn't age well. Delaying makes everything worse. A mediocre decision now beats a perfect decision too late.

2. **Get the information.** But recognize you'll never have complete information. Set a decision deadline and honor it.

3. **Decide.** Commit fully. Once decided, act as if it's the right choice. Ambivalence destroys execution.

4. **Accept consequences.** You'll be wrong sometimes. Accept it and correct course. Don't relitigate.

5. **Use the advisor test.** Ask: "What would I recommend if I were an advisor to this company, not the CEO?" This removes emotional attachment and ego from the decision.

### Speed vs. Quality

```
10% better decision with 50% more time = bad trade
```

In crisis, speed matters more than perfection. Make the best decision with available information. Move on. Correct course as needed.

## Layoffs: Doing It Right

There's no way to make layoffs not painful. But there are many ways to make them worse.

### The Protocol

**1. Get your head right first.** You're not the victim here. The people losing jobs are. Stop feeling sorry for yourself.

**2. Don't delay.** The moment you know layoffs are coming, act fast. Delaying leaks, rumors spread, trust evaporates. Speed is mercy.

**3. Be clear about why.** "Company performance, not your performance." Own the failure as leadership.

**4. Train your managers.** They should deliver the news face-to-face. Not you for everyone, not HR, not email. But train them first.

**5. Be generous.** Severance, references, job search help. Go beyond what's required.

**6. Be present.** Walk the floor. Be available. Don't hide in your office.

**7. Address the survivors.** They're traumatized too. Address their fears directly. Tell them what you know, what you don't, and what you're doing.

### What to Say

"We [leadership] failed. We didn't hit our targets. As a result, we have to reduce the size of the company. This has nothing to do with your performance. I'm sorry."

### What NOT to Say

- "This is actually an opportunity..."
- "We're rightsizing..."
- "This will make us stronger..."
- Anything that minimizes the impact or uses corporate euphemisms

## Firing Executives

Different from layoffs. This is about performance or fit failure.

### Common Reasons

1. **Hired wrong** — Wrong skills, wrong style, wrong level
2. **Company evolved** — Role outgrew the person
3. **Performance** — Not delivering results
4. **Cultural mismatch** — Right skills, wrong values

### The Audit

When questioning an executive, ask:
1. Does the person know what to do?
2. Can the person do it?
3. Is the person capable of growing into the role?

If no to any, move quickly.

### How to Handle It

- **Don't drag it out.** Once you know, act within days.
- **Be direct.** "This isn't working. Today is your last day."
- **Be generous.** Good severance, job search help, positive reference where honest.
- **Control the narrative.** Announce quickly, explain briefly, move on.
- **Find a dignified exit.** "Pursuing other opportunities" is fine.

## The Lead Bullet Trap

When things go wrong, you'll look for a silver bullet — the one hire, partnership, or pivot that will save everything.

**The truth**: There is no silver bullet. Only lead bullets — doing the hard work across every area simultaneously.

**Ask:** "If I'm honest, what are all the things we need to fix?" Then fix them all. The answer is almost always "many things, all at once."

## CEO Psychology

### The Loneliness Problem

You can't tell employees the company might fail. You can't show weakness publicly. But you need to process.

**Solutions:**
- CEO coach or therapist
- Other founders in the same situation
- Board member you trust completely
- Spouse/partner who can handle it
- Peer group (YC, founder communities)

### Embrace the Suck

Some situations are just terrible. There's no reframe that makes them good.

**Don't pretend otherwise.** Acknowledge reality, then deal with it. Toxic positivity in crisis destroys trust faster than the crisis itself.

## Crisis Communication

### To Your Team

1. **Be honest** about what you know and what you don't
2. **Be specific** about what you're doing and why
3. **Be available** for questions
4. **Don't sugarcoat** — people can handle truth better than uncertainty
5. **Show the path forward** — crisis needs to feel navigable, not infinite

### To Your Board

1. **Surface bad news early** — never let the board be surprised
2. **Present the problem AND your plan** — don't just report problems
3. **Ask for specific help** — boards work best with specific requests
4. **Update frequently** — silence makes boards nervous

## Crisis Checklist

When facing company crisis:

- [ ] Don't panic (at least not publicly)
- [ ] Assess honestly — how bad is it really?
- [ ] Identify the 1-2 things that matter most RIGHT NOW
- [ ] Communicate clearly to team (what you know, what you don't, what you're doing)
- [ ] Make decisions fast, even with incomplete information
- [ ] Accept you'll make mistakes — correct them quickly
- [ ] Take care of yourself enough to function
- [ ] Check: are you looking for silver bullets? Use lead bullets instead
- [ ] Remember: every great company faced moments like this

## Horowitz's Laws

1. **All good companies are different; all bad companies are the same.**
2. **The only thing that prepares you to run a company is running a company.**
3. **If you're going to eat shit, don't nibble.** When facing hard things, do it fast and completely.
4. **Take care of the people, the products, and the profits — in that order.**
5. **In business, there's no magic move.** Just lead bullets.

## Anti-Patterns

**The Ostrich** — Avoiding hard decisions hoping the problem resolves itself. It never does. It compounds.

**Peacetime in Wartime** — Running consensus processes and exploring options when the building is on fire. Speed and decisiveness are survival.

**The Silver Bullet Fantasy** — Believing one hire, one deal, one pivot will fix everything. It's always lead bullets — many small fixes across every dimension.

**Public Processing** — Working through your anxiety in front of the team. Process privately, lead publicly.

**The Nibbler** — Doing layoffs in small rounds over months instead of one decisive action. Each round destroys more trust than the last.
