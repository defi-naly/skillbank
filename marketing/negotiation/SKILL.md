# Negotiation

## When to Use

- Closing deals, partnerships, or vendor agreements
- Handling price objections or scope negotiations
- Navigating difficult conversations with stakeholders
- Resolving conflicts between team members or partners
- Preparing for any conversation where interests diverge

## Core Principle: Tactical Empathy

Negotiation is not about logic — it's about emotions and the other party feeling understood. Tactical empathy means understanding the feelings and mindset of another in the moment, and hearing what's behind those feelings.

**Goal**: Get them to say "That's right" (they feel understood), not "You're right" (they want you to go away).

**Foundation**: Before you can influence anyone, they must feel heard. Influence follows understanding, never the reverse.

## The Tactical Toolkit

### 1. Mirroring

Repeat the last 1-3 critical words of what someone said, with upward inflection. Then go silent.

**Why it works**: Creates connection, encourages elaboration, buys thinking time.

```
Them: "We can't do that price."
You:  "Can't do that price?"
Them: [Explains the real constraint]
```

Use silence after mirroring. Let them fill the space. Most people are uncomfortable with silence and will elaborate, often revealing the real issue.

### 2. Labeling

Name the emotion or dynamic you observe. Format: "It seems like..." / "It sounds like..." / "It looks like..."

**Why it works**: Diffuses negative emotions, reinforces positive ones. Neurologically, labeling an emotion reduces its intensity.

- "It seems like you're frustrated with the timeline."
- "It sounds like this decision has a lot riding on it."
- "It looks like you've been burned by vendors before."

**Never say "I understand"** — it's dismissive. Label specifically instead.

### 3. The Accusation Audit

List every negative thing the counterpart could think about you, upfront, before they can raise it.

**Format**: "You probably think [negative assumption]. You might be wondering [concern]. It may seem like [bad interpretation]."

**Why it works**: Defuses objections before they're raised. Anchors expectations low so reality exceeds them. Builds trust through radical transparency.

**Cold outreach example:**
> "You're probably thinking this is another sales pitch. You might be wondering why I'm reaching out when you already have a solution. You may even think I haven't done my research..."

### 4. Calibrated Questions

Open-ended questions starting with "How" or "What" that make them solve your problem for you.

**Power questions:**
- "How am I supposed to do that?"
- "What's the biggest challenge you face?"
- "How does this fit into your priorities?"
- "What happens if we don't do this?"
- "How would you like me to proceed?"

**Avoid "Why"** — it sounds accusatory. Reframe: not "Why did you do that?" but "What caused you to do that?"

### 5. Strategic "No"

Getting to "No" is often more valuable than getting to "Yes."

- "No" makes people feel safe and in control
- A fast "Yes" is often fake (just trying to end conversation)
- "No" surfaces real objections you can address

**Technique**: Ask questions designed to get "No":
- "Is it a ridiculous idea to...?"
- "Have you given up on this project?"
- "Would it be a bad idea if...?"

Conversely, use the "Yes" ladder from Carnegie — start with points of agreement to build momentum. Small yeses compound. The Socratic method works: ask questions that lead to agreement, step by step.

## The Ackerman Pricing Model

A structured approach to price negotiation:

| Step | Action | Amount |
|------|--------|--------|
| 1 | Set your target price | 100% |
| 2 | First offer | 65% of target |
| 3 | Second offer | 85% of target |
| 4 | Third offer | 95% of target |
| 5 | Final offer | 100% (exact, non-round number) |

**Rules:**
- Decreasing increments signal you're approaching your limit
- Non-round final number ($47,350 not $47,000) implies precision and research
- Pivot to non-monetary terms when stuck: scope, timeline, payment terms, exclusivity
- Let them anchor first when you have less information

## Finding Black Swans

Every negotiation has 3-5 pieces of information that would change everything if discovered.

**How to find them:**
- Listen for inconsistencies between words and emotions
- Ask "What would make this a perfect deal for you?"
- Pay attention to what they don't say
- Notice when emotion doesn't match words
- Explore with genuine curiosity, not interrogation

**Common black swans:**
- They have a deadline you don't know about
- There's internal politics constraining them
- They value something you haven't surfaced
- They have an alternative you're unaware of

## Building Rapport Before Asking

Before any negotiation, invest in the relationship. People do business with people they like.

**Carnegie's principles for negotiators:**
- Become genuinely interested in the other person
- Talk in terms of their interests, not yours
- Make the other person feel important — sincerely
- Remember their name and use it naturally
- Let them do most of the talking

**The sequence**: Rapport → Understanding → Influence. Skip rapport and you lose the other two.

## Written Negotiation (Email/Proposals)

- Open with accusation audit to defuse objections
- Use labeling language: "You might be thinking..."
- Frame in terms of their interests, not yours
- End with a calibrated question to invite dialogue
- Keep it short — long emails don't get read
- Appeal to nobler motives: "Given your reputation for..."

## Application by Context

### Pricing/Sales
1. Lead with accusation audit
2. Use calibrated questions to understand their constraints
3. Anchor high, concede strategically using Ackerman
4. Find black swans: "What would make this a no-brainer?"

### Conflict Resolution
1. Mirror to slow down and gather information
2. Label emotions before addressing substance
3. Aim for "That's right" before proposing solutions
4. Use calibrated questions to make them own the solution

### Partnership/Deal Negotiation
1. Discover black swans early through deep listening
2. Use "How am I supposed to do that?" to push back softly
3. Get them to "No" on unreasonable terms
4. Aim for implementation: "How will we make sure this works?"

## Quick Reference

| Technique | Format | Use When |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| Mirroring | Repeat 1-3 words + silence | Need info, build rapport |
| Labeling | "It seems like..." | Emotion present, need to diffuse |
| Accusation Audit | List negatives upfront | Cold outreach, tension expected |
| Calibrated Questions | "How/What..." | Need them to solve your problem |
| Getting to No | "Would it be terrible if..." | "Yes" feels cheap or fake |
| Ackerman | 65% → 85% → 95% → 100% | Price negotiation |
| Yes Ladder | Start with agreement points | Building momentum |

## Anti-Patterns

**Splitting the difference** — Compromising leaves value on the table. No one is satisfied. Find creative solutions instead.

**Defending price immediately** — Shows weakness. Mirror, label, then ask calibrated questions.

**Talking too much** — You learn nothing, they don't feel heard. The person who talks most feels best about the conversation but controls least.

**Treating negotiation as adversarial** — Win-lose framing destroys long-term relationships. You can be firm on substance while being warm on people.

**Logical arguments against emotional positions** — People are creatures of emotion, not logic. Address feelings first, facts second.
