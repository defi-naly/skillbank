# Positioning and Messaging

## When to Use

- Launching a new product and defining how to talk about it
- Messaging isn't resonating — users don't understand what you do
- Repositioning against new competitors or market shifts
- Writing homepage copy, pitch decks, or marketing materials
- Differentiating in a crowded market
- Evaluating whether your current positioning is actually positioning

## Core Principle: Positioning Is Not What You Say — It's What They Hear

Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how you are the best in the world at providing something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about. It's not taglines or slogans — it's the strategic context that makes your value obvious.

## The Positioning Framework

### Step 1: Identify Your Best Customers

Not all customers — your BEST customers. The ones who buy fastest, churn least, and get the most value.

**Questions:**
- Who are our happiest, most successful customers?
- What do they have in common?
- What problem were they trying to solve when they found us?
- What alternatives did they consider?

**Warning:** If you try to position for everyone, you position for no one.

### Step 2: Understand the Competitive Alternatives

What would your best customers do if you didn't exist? This is your real competition — not just direct competitors.

**Common alternatives:**
- Direct competitors
- Different category solutions (spreadsheets vs. project management tool)
- Manual processes or "do nothing"
- In-house solutions

### Step 3: Articulate Your Unique Attributes

What do you have that the alternatives don't? Be honest and specific.

**Test:** Can a competitor truthfully claim the same thing? If yes, it's not unique.

### Step 4: Map Attributes to Customer Value

Attributes are features. Value is what those features enable. Customers buy value, not attributes.

| Attribute | → Value |
|-----------|---------|
| Real-time sync | → Team always has latest version, fewer errors |
| AI-powered analysis | → Decisions in minutes, not days |
| Open source | → No vendor lock-in, full customization |

### Step 5: Find Your Market Category

The category tells customers what you are so they know how to evaluate you. Choose a category where your strengths matter most.

**Three options:**
1. **Existing category** — "We're a better X" (CRM, project management, etc.)
2. **Subcategory** — "We're X for Y" (CRM for freelancers)
3. **New category** — Create a new label entirely (risky but high reward)

## The Fluff Test

Borrowed from Rumelt's strategy work: if your positioning uses inflated language that could apply to any company, it's fluff.

**Fluff indicators:**
- "We leverage our synergistic capabilities..."
- "Best-in-class solution for modern enterprises..."
- "Innovative platform that empowers..."
- Language that sounds impressive but says nothing specific

**The test:** Replace your company name with a competitor's. If the statement still works, it's not positioning.

**Fix:** Use plain language. State what you do, for whom, and why it matters — in words a smart 12-year-old would understand.

## Differentiation Hierarchy

Not all differentiation is equal. From weakest to strongest:

| Level | Type | Durability |
|-------|------|-----------|
| 1 | **Price** — We're cheaper | Weakest — race to bottom |
| 2 | **Features** — We have X that they don't | Weak — easily copied |
| 3 | **Experience** — Using us feels different | Medium — hard to copy well |
| 4 | **Brand/Trust** — People trust us more | Strong — takes years to build |
| 5 | **System** — Our activities reinforce each other | Strongest — can't copy one piece |

**Strategy:** Build differentiation at the highest level you can sustain. Feature differentiation alone is a treadmill.

## Writing Positioning Statements

### The Formula

**For** [target customer] **who** [statement of need], **[product]** is a **[category]** that **[key benefit]**. **Unlike** [competitive alternative], **we** [primary differentiation].

### Homepage Messaging Architecture

```
1. HEADLINE: What you do + who it's for (8 words or fewer)
2. SUBHEAD: The primary value proposition (one sentence)
3. PROOF: Social proof, metrics, or demonstration
4. HOW: 3-4 key capabilities/features
5. CTA: Clear next action
```

**Rules:**
- Lead with the customer's problem, not your solution
- Use the words your customers use, not your internal jargon
- One message per section — don't layer competing ideas
- Every claim should be substantiated or demonstrable

## Narrative and Storytelling

Positioning is the strategy. Narrative is how you bring it to life.

### Story Structure for Marketing

1. **The world as it is** — the problem, the pain, the status quo
2. **Why it matters** — the stakes, what's at risk
3. **The insight** — what most people miss, your contrarian truth
4. **The new way** — your solution, framed as inevitable
5. **The proof** — evidence it works (customers, data, demonstrations)
6. **The invitation** — clear call to action

### The Contrarian Truth

The most memorable positioning stakes a claim that smart people disagree with.

**Test:** Can you articulate what you believe that most of your market does not? If your positioning is consensus, your results will be consensus.

**Examples:**
- Basecamp: "Most project management tools have too many features. You need less, not more."
- Stripe: "Payments infrastructure should be developer-first."
- Apple (early): "Computers should be beautiful and simple, not just powerful."

## Audience-First Writing

### The Empathy Map

Before writing any messaging:

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| What does the reader already believe? | |
| What are they afraid of? | |
| What do they want to be true? | |
| What words do they use to describe this problem? | |
| What have they already tried? | |
| What would make them skeptical of us? | |

### Tone Calibration

Match your tone to your audience and positioning:

| Audience | Tone |
|----------|------|
| Developers | Direct, technical, no BS |
| Enterprise | Professional, risk-reducing, proof-heavy |
| Consumers | Emotional, aspirational, simple |
| Creators | Authentic, empowering, peer-to-peer |

## Diagnosing Messaging Problems

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| "What do you do?" confusion | Category unclear or too novel | Anchor to a known category |
| "How are you different?" | Differentiation is weak or feature-level | Move up the differentiation hierarchy |
| High traffic, low conversion | Value proposition unclear | Lead with customer problem, not solution |
| Attracts wrong customers | Positioning is too broad | Narrow to best-fit customer segment |
| Sounds like competitors | Fluff, no contrarian truth | Run the fluff test, find your contrarian position |

## Application Checklist

- [ ] Identify your best customers (not all customers)
- [ ] List the real competitive alternatives (including "do nothing")
- [ ] Articulate unique attributes honestly
- [ ] Map each attribute to specific customer value
- [ ] Choose a market category where your strengths matter most
- [ ] Run the fluff test on all positioning language
- [ ] State your contrarian truth — what do you believe that the market doesn't?
- [ ] Write positioning statement using the formula
- [ ] Validate messaging uses customer language, not internal jargon
- [ ] Ensure messaging leads with their problem, not your solution

## Anti-Patterns

**Positioning by Feature List** — "We have AI, integrations, and real-time analytics." So does everyone else. Position on the value those features create for specific customers.

**The Category Trap** — Creating a new category when an existing one would serve better. New categories require massive education spend. Only create one when no existing category captures your differentiated value.

**Inside-Out Messaging** — Writing from your perspective ("We built...") instead of the customer's ("You struggle with..."). Flip every sentence to start with them.

**The Everything Page** — Trying to address every segment, use case, and objection on one page. Each audience needs its own message. One page, one audience, one action.

**Consensus Positioning** — If every competitor could say the same thing, you haven't positioned. Real positioning makes some people say "not for me" — that's a feature, not a bug.
